Methodology & Sources

FIRE Number = Annual Expenses ÷ Safe Withdrawal Rate. At a 4% SWR this collapses to the classic “25× rule” (Bengen 1994; Cooley, Hubbard, Walz 1998). The inflation-adjusted target multiplies the today-dollar FIRE number by (1 + inflation)years so you can see what the figure looks like in the future. Years-at-current-rate solves for the year at which your current savings + monthly contributions (compounded at the expected return) equal the inflation-drifted FIRE target — a numerical bisection because both sides of the equation grow with time. Monthly contribution needed is the closed-form solve for the monthly amount that hits the inflation-adjusted target at your chosen year, given your current savings.

This calculator intentionally does not model: sequence-of-returns risk in early retirement (a bear market in years 1-5 of withdrawal hits hardest because you’re selling at lows), tax drag inside taxable accounts, healthcare in early retirement (especially pre-Medicare USA), or variable spending in retirement. Returns are flat-compounded at the rate you set. If you’re planning a 50+ year FIRE retirement, many practitioners use 3.0-3.5% SWR instead of 4% to widen the safety margin — try the slider.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the 4% rule (Trinity Study)?
The 4% rule comes from William Bengen’s 1994 paper and was confirmed by the Trinity Study (Cooley, Hubbard, Walz 1998). Both showed that a portfolio of 50-75% stocks and 25-50% bonds could sustain inflation-adjusted withdrawals of 4% of the starting balance over a 30-year retirement with very low failure risk across every historical 30-year window since 1926. Multiply your annual expenses by 25 and you have your FIRE number. The rule’s elegance hides three big assumptions: the 30-year horizon (FIRE retirements are often 40-60 years), the 1926-1995 US data window (favourable returns by global standards), and the constant-real-dollar withdrawal (no spending flexibility in down years). For longer FIRE horizons many planners use 3.0-3.5% instead.
FIRE vs Lean FIRE vs Fat FIRE — what’s the difference?
Lean FIRE means modest spending in retirement (typically US$30-50k a year, 0.5-0.7× a normal middle-class lifestyle). Fat FIRE means generous spending (US$120k+ a year, 2.0× or more). Regular FIRE sits in between. The cheaper you live, the smaller your FIRE number: Lean FIRE on $30,000 a year is $750,000; Regular FIRE on $80,000 a year is $2,000,000; Fat FIRE on $150,000 a year is $3,750,000 (all at 4% SWR). The trap is targeting Lean because it looks closer — lifestyle inflation, healthcare costs, or wanting kids later turn many Lean-FIRE plans into Regular-FIRE plans halfway through, with the lost compounding being brutal to recover.
Should I use 3% or 4% for my withdrawal rate?
It depends on your retirement length, asset allocation, and flexibility. The 4% rule was calibrated on 30-year retirements; FIRE retirements are often 40-60 years, where bond-heavy portfolios can hit failure rates of 5-10% at 4%. Wade Pfau and Michael Kitces both argue 3.5% is closer to the true safe rate for 40+ years; the Mr. Money Mustache / r/financialindependence communities still mostly default to 4% on the basis that flexibility (cutting spending in bad years) provides enough margin. Practical rule: if your portfolio is > 75% stocks and you can flex your spending 10-15% in down years, 4% is reasonable. If you’re bond-heavy or can’t flex spending, drop to 3.25-3.5%. Run both in this calculator to see the dollar difference — it’s usually $200-400k.
How does inflation change my FIRE number?
It changes the dollar figure but not the strategy. Your FIRE number is denominated in today’s purchasing power; inflation just means the same number of real dollars buys fewer goods in 20 years. If you need $40k a year today, you need $40k of equivalent purchasing power in 2046 — which at 3% inflation is about $72k of 2046 dollars. The same logic applies to the FIRE number itself: a $1M FIRE number today is roughly $1.81M in 2046 dollars at 3% inflation. The 4%-rule safe-withdrawal math assumes you increase withdrawals annually with CPI, so as long as you do that, the 25× rule is inflation-neutral — you don’t need to inflate the target separately. The inflation-adjusted line in this calculator is for psychological calibration: don’t panic when you see $1.8M as your “future” number; that’s the same purchasing power as $1M today.
What’s Coast FIRE versus my FIRE number?
Coast FIRE is the lower bar: enough invested today that, with zero further contributions, compound growth alone gets you to your regular FIRE number by traditional retirement age (say 65). The math: Coast Number = FIRE Number ÷ (1 + return)(65 − current age). At 7% real returns, the Coast number for a 30-year-old targeting $1M at 65 is about $115,000. Hit that and you can downshift — work less, change careers, take a sabbatical — knowing the existing balance will compound to FIRE without your help. Use our Coast FIRE calculator to find your specific Coast number. Both numbers serve different psychological purposes: the FIRE number is the exit door; the Coast number is the “I can stop sprinting” door.
When should I update my FIRE number?
Annually — not more, not less. Re-running the calc every month is anxiety-inducing and creates lifestyle-inflation drift (you’ll catch yourself revising spending up). Once a year, ideally at tax-prep time, refresh four inputs: actual annual expenses (look at last year’s spending, not last month’s budget), updated return-and-inflation assumptions (CPI is published monthly; check your real-return assumption against current 10-year inflation breakevens), retirement-spending plan (the older you get, the more healthcare looms; add 10-20% to base spending for the post-65 budget), and SWR (drop from 4% toward 3.5% as your retirement horizon shortens, paradoxically, because shorter horizons mean less compounding-to-recover-from-a-down-year buffer). Major life events — marriage, kids, home purchase, divorce — warrant an off-cycle update too.
